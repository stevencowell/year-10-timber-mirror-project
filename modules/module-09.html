Theory Block 9

Evaluation

Evaluation is a practical skill. It means judging the finished project honestly and using evidence.

Key Idea

Good evaluation names strengths, weaknesses and improvements.

A useful evaluation does not just say the project is good. It explains how well the mirror meets the design brief, how accurate the construction is, what problems were solved and what would be improved next time.

Why It Matters

Evaluation turns the project into evidence of learning. It shows what you noticed, fixed and would improve next time.

Evaluation Prompts

  • Does the mirror meet the design brief?
  • Are the joints tight and square?
  • Is the finish smooth and even?
  • Is the mirror fitted safely?
  • What was the hardest problem you solved?
  • What would you change if you made it again?

Strong Portfolio Thinking

Use photos and short captions as evidence. A clear photo with one honest sentence is usually better than a giant paragraph of waffle.

Final Evidence

Evaluate What Can Be Seen

Students should link their evaluation to visible evidence: square frame, neat corners, smooth finish, safe fitting and one realistic improvement.

Evaluation Criteria

A fair evaluation uses criteria instead of feelings. Students should judge the mirror against the design brief, the quality of construction and the evidence gathered during the build.

Criterion Evidence To Check Useful Question
Function Mirror fits securely, hangs or stands safely, frame supports the glass. Does it do the job it was designed to do?
Accuracy Square corners, matching lengths, neat joints, even rebate. Where can I see accurate measuring and marking?
Finish Smooth sanding, no visible glue marks, even finish, clean edges. What would a viewer notice first?
Process Photos, process log, problem solving notes, teacher feedback. Can I prove how the project improved over time?
Sustainability Waste reduction, timber choice, safe disposal of finish materials. Did I use materials responsibly?

Strong Evaluation Structure

A strong evaluation paragraph should name the judgment, give evidence, and explain the improvement. For example: the frame is mostly square because the diagonals were checked during dry fit, but one corner has a small gap because the tenon shoulder was not cut cleanly. Next time, the joint should be test-fitted earlier and pared more carefully before glue-up.

  • Use specific words: square, flush, tight, even, secure, aligned, rough, chipped.
  • Link comments to photos or teacher feedback.
  • Include one real improvement, not a fake one like work harder.
  • Explain how the skill could transfer to another timber project.

Use both visible and process evidence

Visible evidence

Use the finished mirror to judge closed or gapped joints, square corners, flush surfaces, routed profiles, sanding, finish, the rebate and secure mirror fitting.

Process evidence

Use photos, cutting plans, dry-fit checks, diagonal measurements, test cuts, teacher feedback and notes about corrections to explain how the project developed.

A finished project can look successful while hiding a weak process. A fair evaluation uses both kinds of evidence and identifies one genuine next-step improvement.
